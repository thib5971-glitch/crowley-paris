The story

At 18 rue Saint-Lazare, in the 9th arrondissement, Charles Bodinier (1844-1911), former secretary general of the Comédie-Française who had run the Théâtre d'Application since 1888, opened a small hall in 1890 that soon became known as La Bodinière. It lived mainly on “matinées-causeries”: afternoon lectures, often followed by recitals, before an audience of aristocrats, wealthy bourgeois and intellectuals. Anatole France, François Coppée, Maurice Donnay and Francisque Sarcey spoke there; Robert de Montesquiou gave a lecture there on 17 January 1894 with the participation of Sarah Bernhardt, and Yvette Guilbert sang there in 1891 between two talks by Hugues Le Roux. Shadow-theatre shows were also staged there.

The venue was no stranger to esoteric circles. In March 1892 Paul Fort's Théâtre d'Art presented its last programme there, which included Les Noces de Sathan by Jules Bois, a writer close to MacGregor Mathers; the evening was a failure. A few years later, at Jules Bois's suggestion, the Matherses moved their “Rites of Isis” there, Egyptianising ceremonies begun on avenue Mozart; in January 1900 L'Écho de Paris announced them as mere “lectures”, hence their semi-public character: MacGregor played the hierophant Rameses, Moina the high priestess Anari. These performances helped make the Parisian Golden Dawn known beyond the circle of initiates.

Crowley himself would write to Gerald Kelly that he had seen the Matherses celebrate their Rites of Isis at La Bodinière. According to Tobias Churton, he may have discovered them as early as a stop in Paris after the summer of 1899, and saw them again during the eight days he spent in Paris in January 1900. It was at one of these sessions that he fell for the New York singer Lucile Hill, who in May 1900 would be Susan Strong's understudy in Tannhäuser at Covent Garden. Crowley, initiated into the Golden Dawn in London in November 1898, rose rapidly through the order's first grades during 1899, before being received by Mathers in Paris in January 1900.

La Bodinière closed in 1902. The premises at 18 rue Saint-Lazare are now occupied by a restaurant; the sources report no visible trace of the former lecture hall.
