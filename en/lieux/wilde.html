The story

In August 1912, Jacob Epstein's funerary monument to Oscar Wilde arrived at Père-Lachaise. Robert Ross, Wilde's literary executor, oversaw its installation. But the official inauguration was delayed: Epstein refused to alter the outsized testicles of the flying figure, carved in Derbyshire limestone, as propriety demanded. Clay was added to them, which only drew more attention. In a fine historical irony, Wilde's monument ended up smothered under a tarpaulin.

Crowley may have heard about it from the artist Nina Hamnett, who had come to Paris with Epstein and his wife. Shocked by the charge of indecency, the three of them would go to Père-Lachaise every afternoon to pull the tarpaulin off, until the day policemen hidden behind the tombs rushed out to put it back.

Crowley then sent Parisian newspapers a manifesto, "In the Name of the Liberty of Art". It proclaimed the artist's right to create what he wants and announced that he would unveil the monument himself, "already mutilated and degraded by order of the Prefect of the Seine". The meeting was set for midday on Wednesday 5 November 1913, in front of the tomb. Gil Blas printed the notice that very day, on page 4 of its "Arts" section, promising to report on the demonstration the next day. It never did.

The day before, Crowley told Monsieur Bourcier, on rue Vavin, about his plan; Bourcier predicted a line of bayonets. So Crowley enlisted an "enthusiastic young American", possibly Michael Brenner. At closing time, the two men attached fine wires to the tarpaulin, and Crowley cut the cords until they held by a single thread. The plan was for Crowley to give his speech from a distance; at his signal, the American, hidden in a tree some 200 metres away, would pull the wires.

On 5 November, Crowley led a gathering of enthusiasts in solemn procession. Patrols scattered at the sight of them and he expected a spectacular arrest. But no one was waiting at the tomb: the police had evidently been ordered not to bother the "mad Irish poet". In dreary drizzle, Crowley gave his speech and unveiled Epstein's work. He would call it a "disheartening success". Yet the affair made a splash in the French and British press, and as far as Chicago.

Epstein, for his part, distanced himself in The Times of 8 November 1913, writing that he would have preferred the monument to stay veiled until the "improvements" made against his wishes had been removed.

Later, Ross wanted his official inauguration at all costs. He had a bronze butterfly, made by another artist, fixed over the disputed detail as a cache-sexe, and went ahead despite Epstein's refusal to attend. Returning to check whether the tarpaulin had been put back, Crowley found the addition hilarious. The butterfly was the emblem of Whistler, Wilde's legendary sparring partner in wit, which made its presence a supreme insult. Crowley seized it and walked out without the gatekeeper noticing. In London he made his entrance at the Café Royal in evening dress, the butterfly worn where modesty required. Epstein was there, and Crowley would remember a "glorious evening".

Biographers long misdated this first exploit: Martin Booth put it in 1912, others in August 1914. The error comes from the Confessions, where Crowley tells the episode undated and out of chronological order. It was Tobias Churton who found the Gil Blas notice and restored the date of 5 November 1913.

The monument has kept drawing gestures of devotion. Since 2011 its lower part has been protected by glass, because cleaning off the lipstick left by admirers was damaging the stone. Churton sees in this a fresh irony Crowley would have relished.

Every year on 5 November, members of Ordo Templi Orientis gather at Wilde's tomb to celebrate this event, in the name of artistic freedom and sexual freedom.
