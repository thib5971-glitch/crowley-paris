Aleister Crowley 1875-1947

An English poet, mountaineer and occultist from a wealthy family, Edward Alexander, known as Aleister Crowley, joined the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn in 1898. In Cairo in 1904 he received the Book of the Law, the founding text of Thelema, summed up in the formula “Do what thou wilt shall be the whole of the Law”. In 1907, with George Cecil Jones, he founded the A∴A∴, an initiatory order devoted to the Great Work. In 1925 he was recognised as head of the Ordo Templi Orientis.

Paris became a second home to him. Mathers initiated him there as Adeptus Minor in January 1900; in 1902-1903 he moved in Gerald Kelly's English-speaking circle in Montparnasse, met Rodin and Somerset Maugham, then married Rose, Kelly's sister. Made a Freemason in Paris in 1904, he took the hôtel de Blois, rue Vavin, as his home base from 1908: there he held his magical retreat of October 1908, then the Paris Working with Victor Neuburg at the turn of 1914, shortly after unveiling Oscar Wilde's tomb.

Expelled from his Abbey at Cefalù by Mussolini in 1923, he returned to Paris in January 1924 and made it the centre of his work until 1929. There he pursued his magical work, wrote, gathered disciples and prepared his great publishing projects, among them Magick in Theory and Practice, published in Paris in 1929. Leah Hirsig, Dorothy Olsen, Kasimira Bass and then Maria de Miramar shared his life in turn. In March 1929 the Préfecture did not renew his residence; on 17 April he took the train for Brussels at the Gare du Nord, and his Paris years came to an end.

Samuel Liddell MacGregor Mathers 1854-1918

Co-founder of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn in 1888 and translator of The Kabbalah Unveiled and the Book of Abramelin, Mathers settled in Paris in 1892 with his wife Moina. There he founded the Ahathoor temple and ran the Order from the French capital.

He initiated Crowley into the grade of Adeptus Minor in Paris in January 1900, against the wishes of the London adepts. The Golden Dawn revolt, and then the quarrel between the two men, followed soon after. Mathers died in Paris in 1918.

Moina Mathers 1865-1928

Born Mina Bergson, sister of the philosopher Henri Bergson, she studied painting at the Slade School in London before marrying MacGregor Mathers in 1890. She decorated the Ahathoor temple and, in 1899, performed with him the “Rites of Isis”, staged publicly in Paris.

After her husband’s death, she led in London the order he had founded after the schism, the Alpha et Omega, until her death in 1928.

Rose Edith Kelly 1874-1932

Sister of the painter Gerald Kelly, she married Crowley in August 1903. In Cairo, in March 1904, she told him the gods were waiting for him: this was the starting point for the reception of the Book of the Law, which he wrote down from inner dictation from 8 to 10 April.

They had two daughters, Nuit Ma Ahathoor, who died in 1906, and Lola Zaza, born in 1907. The couple divorced in 1909. It was in Paris, in 1902, that Gerald Kelly introduced her to Crowley, on rue Campagne-Première.

Gerald Kelly 1879-1972

A friend of Crowley’s since Cambridge, the young painter settled in Paris in 1901 and rented a studio on rue Campagne-Première. He introduced Crowley to the Montparnasse art scene, to the Chat Blanc, and to Rodin.

His sister Rose married Crowley in 1903. A renowned portraitist, Kelly was knighted in 1945 and served as President of the Royal Academy from 1949 to 1954.

Auguste Rodin 1840-1917

The most famous sculptor of his day received Crowley, introduced by Gerald Kelly, in 1902. Crowley visited his studios and wrote a series of poems on his works, published in 1907 as Rodin in Rime, with drawings by the sculptor.

William Somerset Maugham 1874-1965

The future novelist frequented the Chat Blanc, on rue d’Odessa, where he met Crowley at the turn of the century. He used him as the model for the sinister magus Oliver Haddo in his novel The Magician (1908), a portrait Crowley took very badly.

Victor Neuburg 1883-1940

A poet Crowley met at Cambridge, he became his disciple and partner in magical work. From 31 December 1913 to 12 February 1914, they carried out together in Paris a long series of operations, the Paris Working.

After their break in 1914, Neuburg devoted himself to poetry and publishing, and encouraged young writers, among them Dylan Thomas.

Leah Hirsig 1883-1975

A Swiss-born American schoolteacher, she joined Crowley in 1919 and became his “Scarlet Woman”. She shared the adventure of the Abbey of Thelema in Cefalù, from 1920 to 1923.

She stayed in Paris with Crowley in 1924, after their expulsion from Italy, before they parted.

Dorothy Olsen

An American, Crowley’s companion in 1924 and 1925, after Leah Hirsig. She went with him to Paris, where they stayed at the Hôtel du Dauphin among other places, then to North Africa. Little is known of her life before and after this period.

Gerald Yorke 1901-1983

A young Englishman passionate about esotericism, he became Crowley’s pupil and financial backer from 1928, during the last Paris months. He later assembled a vast collection of Crowley’s manuscripts and papers, now held at the Warburg Institute in London.

Papus (Gérard Encausse) 1865-1916

A physician and a leading figure of Parisian occultism, founder of the Martinist Order, he frequented the Matherses and the Ahathoor temple. Crowley counted him among the saints of the Gnostic Mass. He died on 25 October 1916 and is buried at Père-Lachaise.

Oscar Wilde 1854-1900

The Irish writer died in Paris in 1900. His tomb, sculpted by Jacob Epstein, was installed at Père-Lachaise in 1912 and caused a scandal with its nudity. In 1914, Crowley loudly joined the controversy over the monument.

Arnold Bennett 1867-1931

An English novelist born in the Staffordshire Potteries, Arnold Bennett made his name with Anna of the Five Towns and The Grand Babylon Hotel (1902), then The Old Wives' Tale (1908). Settled in Paris in the first years of the century, he kept a journal attentive to its artistic and social circles, and became one of the most widely read English writers of his day.

He met Crowley around 1902-1903, at one of Marcel Schwob's afternoons or in Gerald Kelly's studio, and ran into him at the Chat Blanc. On 22 April 1904 Crowley and his wife Rose invited him by telegram to lunch at Paillard's: his journal records a red waistcoat studded with jewels and an enormous ring. He drew on this for the « Mahatma » of Paris Nights (1913); Crowley returned the compliment, ironically, in his Confessions.

Israel Regardie 1907-1985

Born in London to a Jewish family that later moved to the United States, Francis Israel Regardie became fascinated by Crowley's writings at a very young age and wrote to him from Washington. He would become one of the leading occult authors of the twentieth century: his edition of the Golden Dawn rituals and his study of Crowley, The Eye in the Triangle (1970), remain standard references.

Having accepted board and lodging in exchange for secretarial work, he arrived at the gare Saint-Lazare on 12 October 1928, Crowley's 53rd birthday, and was installed at 55, avenue de Suffren. His sister Sarah had tried to have his visa refused, and the police took an interest in him: on 8 March 1929 he was refused leave to stay and left France with Maria de Miramar. He would remain with Crowley for three years.

Kasimira Bass 1887-?

Born in 1887 in Lemberg (now Lviv), Kasimira Bass came to the United States in 1922 under the name Kasimira de Helleparth, with a daughter from a first marriage in Vienna, and promptly married John F. Bass Jr. In California W. T. Smith told her about Crowley; she travelled to Europe intending to meet him.

Crowley made her acquaintance in the autumn of 1927 and proposed to her, despite an unfavourable answer from the I Ching. She joined him at the hôtel Foyot on 5 March 1928, became his sex-magick partner, followed him to the Royal Condé, the South of France and the Alps, and pledged 10,000 dollars to his publication fund. Living with him on the avenue de Suffren, she fled on 11 October with 5,000 francs, came back on the 23rd, then left for good on 3 November 1928.

Man Ray 1890-1976

Born Emmanuel Radnitzky in Philadelphia, Man Ray was a painter and photographer and a figure of New York Dada alongside Marcel Duchamp. Arriving in Paris in July 1921, he became one of the great photographers of Surrealism and the portraitist of Montparnasse in the Roaring Twenties, inventor of « rayographs », images made without a camera.

In 1922 he moved into a studio at 31 bis, rue Campagne-Première, which he kept for about fourteen years, and in 1923 took on Berenice Abbott as his assistant. Churton lists him, in early 1928, among Crowley's professional contacts in Paris, noting that Crowley had lodged in the same street a quarter of a century earlier. No source says that Crowley sat for him.

Jacob Epstein 1880-1959

Born in New York to Jewish parents from Poland, Jacob Epstein studied in Paris before settling in London in 1905, where he became one of the pioneers of modern sculpture in Britain. His figures, often judged indecent, caused one scandal after another. Robert Ross, Oscar Wilde's literary executor, commissioned him to make the writer's funerary monument.

Carved in Derbyshire limestone, the flying sphinx reached Père-Lachaise in August 1912; Epstein refused to tone down its anatomy, and the work was covered with a tarpaulin on the prefect's orders. On 5 November 1913 Crowley unveiled it in the name of artistic freedom; Epstein disowned the gesture in The Times of 8 November. Later, though, at the Café Royal in London, he witnessed Crowley's entrance sporting the bronze butterfly removed from the monument.

Berenice Abbott 1898-1991

Raised in Ohio, Berenice Abbott left New York for Paris with six dollars in her pocket to study sculpture at the Académie de la Grande Chaumière. As Man Ray's assistant she found her calling: her portraits of artistic Paris made her name, she rescued Eugène Atget's archive, and in the 1930s she photographed New York.

Crowley met her as soon as he arrived in Paris in January 1924, when she had just joined Man Ray's studio at 31 bis, rue Campagne-Première, which she later left to open her own. On 10 November 1926, during the rue Frochot period, she took part with him in a sex-magick operation aimed at health, energy and the journey to Egypt. In January 1927 his diary lists her among his nine Paris mistresses.

Stephen Haweis 1878-1969

Son of the clergyman and essayist Hugh Reginald Haweis and the artist Mary Eliza Haweis, Hugh Stephen Haweis went up to Peterhouse, Cambridge, then trained as a painter at the Académie Julian and the Académie Colarossi. There in 1902 he met Mina Loy, whom he married in December 1903 in an unhappy union. A painter and engraver, he exhibited at the 1904 Salon d'automne and went into partnership with the photographer Henry Coles.

Probably introduced to Crowley by Gerald Kelly, he belonged to the same Anglo-American circle in Montparnasse and lived, like Mina Loy, at 17, rue Campagne-Première. In late 1902 Crowley sent him to sound out Mathers in Montmartre; he came back wearied by a lecture on the gods of Mexico. Rodin, taken with his gum-bichromate prints, made him one of his official photographers in 1904; with Coles he produced the portrait of Crowley used as the frontispiece of his Collected Works (1905).

Sylvia Beach 1887-1962

An American born in Baltimore, Sylvia Beach opened Shakespeare and Company in Paris in November 1919, an English-language bookshop and lending library that later moved to 12, rue de l'Odéon. Hemingway, Pound, Fitzgerald and above all James Joyce frequented it; in 1922 she published Ulysses under the shop's imprint. She closed it during the Occupation, in December 1941.

Arriving from Nice on 5 January 1924, Crowley saw her as early as the 7th to propose that she sell his Confessions by subscription, for a 5 % commission and in the strictest secrecy. She declined, not wanting a reputation for « naughty books »; Crowley quipped that it was the only reputation she had. In her memoirs, published in 1956, she recalls a brief and unpleasant meeting with him and a blonde companion.

Jane Wolfe 1875-1958

An American silent-film actress, Jane Wolfe took an interest in Crowley's writings and joined him in 1920 at the Abbey of Thelema in Cefalù, Sicily, where she undertook a long magical retirement. A faithful disciple of Thelema, she later pursued that path in California for the rest of her life.

Having become his secretary in Tunisia in early 1926, she went to the Pau area with Dorothy Olsen in July, then joined Crowley in Paris and on 12 September took a room in his hotel on the rue Frochot. In November she co-signed a letter from Crowley to Karl Germer. Operated on at the American Hospital in Neuilly at the end of April 1927, where she stayed eleven weeks, she sailed for New York on 1 October 1927.

Karl Germer 1885-1962

A German from Weida in Thuringia, Karl Germer belonged to Heinrich Tränker's Pansophic lodge when he came into contact with Crowley. Won over to Thelema in 1925, he became for decades his main financial backer and most regular correspondent and, after Crowley's death, his successor as head of the Ordo Templi Orientis.

On 25 May 1925 Crowley wrote to him from the hôtel du Dauphin to arrange his journey to Germany; they met on 21 June at Weida, and Germer, won over at the Hohenleuben conference, broke with Tränker. Later settled in the United States, where he worked for the Hamburg-American Line, he sent money to Paris and received a stream of letters and advice in return. In the summer of 1929 he went with Crowley to Leipzig for his marriage to Maria de Miramar.
